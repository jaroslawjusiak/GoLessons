# One-Year Go Course for Beginners

## From First Rules to Approximately 10 Kyu

### Course overview

This syllabus is designed for a local Go club meeting approximately once per week for 30 meetings of about four hours each. It assumes students begin with no knowledge of Go and aims to bring regular participants to roughly 10 kyu strength. Individual progress will vary considerably; reaching 10 kyu requires substantial playing and problem-solving in addition to attending lectures.

Each meeting begins with a focused 10–20 minute lecture. The lecture should introduce only a small number of ideas, preferably demonstrated with several positions rather than explained abstractly. Most of the remaining meeting should be spent playing, reviewing games, and solving problems.

The course deliberately changes board size as students develop:

- Meetings 1–4: 9×9 — rules, capture, life and death, territory.
- Meetings 5–14: primarily 13×13 — connection, shape, fighting, opening principles, attack and defense.
- Meetings 15–30: 19×19 — whole-board strategy, direction of play, joseki, invasions, reductions, aji, sabaki and endgame.

The central teaching principle is that students should learn to recognize reasons for moves rather than memorize sequences. Joseki, opening patterns, and proverbs should therefore be presented as examples of strategic principles rather than rules that must always be followed.

# Part I — Learning the Game on 9×9

## Meeting 1 — Rules, Liberties and Capturing

**Goal:** Students should be able to complete their first legal game.

### Lecture contents

- The board, intersections and stones.
- Black moves first; players alternate.
- Liberties of a stone.
- Strings/groups of connected stones.
- Capturing stones by removing their last liberty.
- Atari.
- Suicide rule.
- Very short introduction to ko.
- The objective of the game.
- Playing until there is nothing useful left to do.
- Basic scoring.

The lecture should contain many tiny capture exercises. Afterward, students should immediately play 9×9 games.

### Further study

- Janice Kim & Jeong Soo-hyun, *Learn to Play Go, Vol. 1: A Master's Guide to the Ultimate Game*
- Cho Chikun, *Go: A Complete Introduction to the Game*
- Online Go Server, [How to Play Go](https://online-go.com/learn-to-play-go)
- [Sensei's Library — Rules of Go](https://senseis.xmp.net/?RulesOfGo)

---

## Meeting 2 — Territory, Boundaries and the End of the Game

**Goal:** Understand what players are trying to build and recognize when a game is finished.

### Lecture contents

- What is territory?
- How stones form boundaries.
- Open versus closed areas.
- Stones versus territory.
- Why simply surrounding the largest possible area does not work.
- Neutral points (dame).
- Dead stones inside territory.
- Passing.
- Determining the winner.
- Brief explanation that different rulesets count slightly differently.

Use finished or nearly finished 9×9 positions and ask students to identify Black's and White's territory.

### Further study

- Janice Kim & Jeong Soo-hyun, *Learn to Play Go, Vol. 1*
- [Sensei's Library — Territory](https://senseis.xmp.net/?Territory)
- [Sensei's Library — Scoring](https://senseis.xmp.net/?Scoring)

## Meeting 3 — Life, Death and Two Eyes

**Goal:** Recognize groups that can and cannot be captured.

### Lecture contents

- Why surrounded stones are not necessarily dead.
- Eyes and false eyes.
- Why two separate eyes guarantee life.
- How to prevent the opponent from making two eyes.
- Killing by occupying vital points.
- Basic corner and side life-and-death positions.
- Introduction to seki (mutual life).
- Difference between "currently alive" and "can probably make life."

This should be highly interactive: show a position and repeatedly ask, "Can Black capture this group?"

### Further study

- James Davies, *Life and Death*
- Richard Bozulich, *Get Strong at Life and Death*
- Kano Yoshinori, *Graded Go Problems for Beginners, Vol. 1*
- [Sensei's Library — Two Eyes](https://senseis.xmp.net/?TwoEyes)
- [Sensei's Library — Life and Death](https://senseis.xmp.net/?LifeAndDeath)

## Meeting 4 — Ko, Capturing Races and Reading

**Goal:** Introduce calculation rather than purely intuitive play.

### Lecture contents

- Review of atari and capture.
- The ko rule and why it exists.
- Ko threats at a beginner's level.
- Capturing races (semeai).
- Counting liberties.
- Shared liberties.
- "What happens if I play here?"
- Reading one, two and three moves ahead.
- Looking at forcing replies before playing.

Introduce the habit of mentally checking every move for captures, ataris and immediate danger.

### Further study

- Kano Yoshinori, *Graded Go Problems for Beginners, Vol. 1*
- [Sensei's Library — Ko](https://senseis.xmp.net/?Ko)
- [Sensei's Library — Semeai](https://senseis.xmp.net/?Semeai)

## Meeting 13 — Fundamental Go Proverbs

**Goal:** Introduce useful heuristics while emphasizing that proverbs have exceptions.

### Lecture contents

Examples to examine:

- Urgent moves before big moves.
- Hane at the head of two stones.
- Hane at the head of three stones.
- Don't push from behind.
- Don't attach to weak stones without a reason.
- Play away from thickness.
- Don't use thickness to make territory.
- Your opponent's good move is often your good move.
- Don't go fishing while your house is on fire.

For every proverb, show one position where it works and, where practical, one where blindly following it fails.

## Meeting 14 — Reviewing a Game and Learning From Mistakes

**Goal:** Teach students how to improve without a teacher beside them.

### Lecture contents

- Reconstructing one's game.
- Finding turning points.
- Distinguishing tactical mistakes from strategic mistakes.
- Looking for the first significant mistake rather than the last.
- Asking "What was I trying to accomplish?"

## Meeting 27 — Reading and Candidate Moves

**Goal:** Develop a repeatable method for deciding what to play.

### Lecture contents

Before playing, consider:

1. Are any groups in immediate danger?
2. Are there captures, cuts or tactical opportunities?
3. What are the opponent's forcing replies?
4. Which groups are weak?
5. What are the largest candidate moves?
6. Which move is urgent rather than merely big?

Then calculate concrete variations rather than relying entirely on intuition.

Introduce reading from the opponent's strongest response, not the response one hopes they will play.

### Further study

- Kano Yoshinori, *Graded Go Problems for Beginners, Vols. 2–3*
- James Davies, *Tesuji*
- James Davies, *Life and Death*

## Meeting 30 — Putting Everything Together: How to Think During a Game

**Goal:** Integrate the year's tactical and strategic concepts into a practical decision-making process.

### Lecture contents

Review the game as a sequence of questions:

- Are all my groups safe?
- Are all the opponent's groups safe?
- Is there an urgent move?
- Where is the biggest area?
- Which group is weakest?
- Should I attack, defend, invade or reduce?
- Where are the cuts and other tactical weaknesses?
- Am I playing toward or away from strength?
- Is my move sente?
- Am I making territory, influence, shape, or some other concrete profit?
- Am I ahead or behind?
- What is my opponent's best response?

End with the principle that stronger Go is largely the accumulation of better judgments rather than knowledge of increasingly complicated sequences.

The remainder of this meeting can be a small tournament followed by collective reviews of several games.

# Recommended Course-Wide Resources

Rather than assigning a completely different book every week, it is useful to establish a small club library from which students can borrow material.

- **Janice Kim & Jeong Soo-hyun — *Learn to Play Go*, Vols. 1–5.** Particularly suitable for the beginning portion of the course.
- **Kano Yoshinori — *Graded Go Problems for Beginners*, Vols. 1–4.** Probably the most useful companion to the entire syllabus. Students should solve problems regularly rather than merely read about Go.
- **Kageyama Toshiro — *Lessons in the Fundamentals of Go*.** Excellent once students know the rules and have some playing experience.
- **Richard Bozulich — *The Second Book of Go*.** A broad bridge between knowing the rules and understanding Go strategy.
- **James Davies — *Tesuji*.** A classic introduction to tactical techniques.
- **James Davies — *Life and Death*.** Useful once basic two-eye life is understood.
- **Ishida Akira & James Davies — *Attack and Defense*.** Especially relevant to the second half of the course.
- **Otake Hideo — *Opening Theory Made Easy*.** Focuses on opening principles rather than excessive memorization.
- **Charles Matthews — *Shape Up!*.** A detailed resource on shape; legally available online from the author/community sources.
- **Sensei's Library — https://senseis.xmp.net/**. A long-running Go community wiki. Particularly useful for looking up terms introduced in class.
- **Online Go Server — https://online-go.com/**. Suitable for playing, reviewing, puzzles and AI-assisted analysis.
- **Dwyrin / BattsGo on YouTube.** The *Back to Basics* games are particularly useful for seeing fundamental principles applied in complete games.
- **Nick Sibicky's Go lectures on YouTube.** A large archive of accessible lectures on shape, joseki, direction of play, fighting and whole-board strategy.
- **Baduk Doctor on YouTube.** Particularly useful once students have moved to 19×19 and want demonstrations of positional judgment, fighting and reading.

Because YouTube playlists, URLs, and video titles can change, searching these channels for the week's concept (for example, "Nick Sibicky sente", "Baduk Doctor direction of play", or "Dwyrin Back to Basics") is preferable to making individual videos required course material.

# Suggested Structure of Each Four-Hour Meeting

A useful recurring format is:

1. **10–20 minutes — lecture.** Introduce one clearly defined concept with positions on a demonstration board.
2. **10–20 minutes — exercises.** Give 3–6 positions directly related to the lecture.
3. **80–100 minutes — games.** Encourage students to consciously apply the week's concept.
4. **15-minute break.**
5. **60–80 minutes — games, teaching games or problem solving.**
6. **30–45 minutes — reviews.** Review a few student games, concentrating on the week's theme rather than correcting every mistake.

During the 9×9 stage, students should play many short games rather than one long game. On 13×13, complete games followed by immediate review become more useful. Once the class reaches 19×19, participants should generally play at least one serious game per meeting.

# Suggested Homework

The most useful homework for this level is deliberately modest. Students should ideally play two or more games between meetings and solve approximately 5–10 minutes of Go problems on most days. Problems should initially be extremely easy: a problem solved correctly in 20 seconds is often more useful for developing pattern recognition than a difficult problem requiring ten minutes.

Students can also keep a simple Go journal containing one position from each serious game and the answer to three questions: "Where did the game change?", "Which of my groups caused me the most trouble?", and "What would I play differently next time?" This creates a useful record of progress over the year.

# Expected Milestones

The kyu estimates should be treated as approximate because ranks differ between clubs and online servers.

- **After Meetings 1–4:** Students know the rules, can finish and score 9×9 games, understand basic life and death, and recognize elementary tactical situations. Approximately 30–25 kyu.
- **After Meetings 5–10:** Students understand connection, cutting, shape, group strength, attack and defense. Approximately 25–20 kyu.
- **After Meetings 11–14:** Students recognize sente, basic tesuji and strategic priorities and can review simple games. Approximately 20–17 kyu.
- **After Meetings 15–20:** Students can play meaningful 19×19 games and understand opening development, influence, joseki principles, frameworks, invasions and reductions. Approximately 17–14 kyu.
- **After Meetings 21–26:** Students begin to understand efficiency, thickness, sophisticated attacking ideas, sabaki, aji and timing. Approximately 14–12 kyu.
- **After Meetings 27–30:** Students have a framework for reading, positional evaluation, counting and endgame play. With sufficient games and problem practice, approximately 12–10 kyu is a reasonable target.

The important distinction is between "topics introduced" and "topics mastered." A 10-kyu player will not have mastered sabaki, aji, direction of play, joseki or yose. The purpose of introducing these concepts is to give students a vocabulary and mental framework that they can continue developing after the course.
